## Bounded investigation for A

Start by writing down the specific user task, where it begins and ends, who performs it, and the outcome the sponsor wants improved. The evidence says this task is bounded but does not supply its details. Preserve that gap until records can establish the boundary. Then define a baseline measure appropriate to the task, such as time, completion rate, or error frequency; these are candidate measures, not observed results.

Check whether the permitted aggregate data can actually support that measure. Permission to use aggregates does not establish their completeness, quality, freshness, or suitability for attributing an improvement. Document missing fields, aggregation limits, and any inability to distinguish the relevant user population. Do not assume access to underlying records or personal data.

Translate the claimed benefit range into explicit assumptions: baseline volume, improvement per task, adoption, and the period over which benefits accrue, where applicable. Label every unverified input and identify evidence needed to narrow the range. Pair this with an updated effort range and dependencies. Avoid treating preliminary estimates as commitments.

The investigation should end with a short decision note containing a supported baseline or a statement that no baseline is yet possible, a revised benefit range with assumptions, data limitations, a bounded intervention hypothesis, and a recommendation to proceed, refine, or stop. Implementation should advance only if evidence supports meaningful value, permitted data is sufficient, and ownership and delivery effort are credible. No numeric threshold is invented here; the sponsor's acceptable investment and success criteria remain to be established.

Reassess all three when evidence changes; the recommendation is not a permanent ranking.
